⚠ Read first — caveats (mostly about the dataset)

  • “Normal” is tumor-adjacent tissue, not healthy donors. TCGA/GEO “normal” samples sit next to the tumor, so they can carry mild early changes. Our “healthy baseline” means adjacent-normal.
  • This is an array, not sequencing. The HM450 chip reads a fixed ~485k CpG set that over-represents gene promoters and certain gene families. This design bias is real and is exactly why broad “pathway” claims did not hold up (see the Atlas section).
  • Methylation predicts, it does not measure, gene activity. “Silencing/activation” are well-supported predictions from promoter methylation — not direct measurements of gene expression.
  • A few individual probes have very few normal samples. We filtered the marker tables to sites with ≥30 tumor and ≥30 normal samples so nothing rests on n=1.

1. What we did

DNA methylation is a chemical “on/off” layer on top of DNA that controls which genes are switched on. We measured it at ~485,000 positions in 888 breast samples, compared tumor vs. normal, found the exact sites and genes the tumor switches off or on, validated the result two independent ways, and mapped the changes onto longevity genes.

In plain EnglishA CpG site is one specific spot on the DNA where methylation can occur. A promoter is a gene’s “on-switch” region. A beta value (β) is how methylated a site is, from 0 (none) to 1 (fully).

4. The volcano plot — and why it splits into two wings

hyper (tumor gains methylation → silencing) hypo (tumor loses methylation → activation) everything else · dashed gold lines = the |Δβ|=0.20 “strong” cutoffs · hover any colored dot for its gene & location.
In plain EnglishWhat it is: every dot is one CpG site. Left–right (Δβ) = how much methylation changed between tumor and normal; up (−log10 FDR) = how confident we are it’s a real difference. Why two wings: the split is the whole point — the left wing is sites the tumor loses methylation at (hypo → genes turned on), the right wing is sites it gains methylation at (hyper → genes turned off). The empty middle-bottom = sites that barely change. What it means: tumors don’t nudge methylation randomly — they make many large, highly-confident changes in both directions. What’s good: dots sit very high (tiny FDR) and far out (big Δβ) in both wings → strong, reliable, two-directional reprogramming. Caveat: the exact height depends on sample size (888 samples makes FDRs extreme), so read the shape and spread, not the absolute height.
Are the statistics done correctly? (yes — here’s how)

For methylation the effect size is Δβ = tumor mean − normal mean (a difference in methylation fraction) — not log-fold-change, which is used for RNA/gene-expression. We verified Δβ equals tumor−normal exactly. Significance is a Welch’s t-test per site, corrected for ~486k simultaneous tests with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR; the y-axis is −log10(FDR). So the axes and tests are the correct ones for array methylation.

6. Did we trust it? Two independent checks

Validation 1 — internal (leakage audit + nested cross-validation). Checked the near-perfect result wasn’t the model “cheating.” After a leakage-proof re-test, accuracy stayed at AUC ≈ 0.996 → the signal is real.
Validation 2 — external cohort (GSE66695). Checked the markers hold in a separate patient group from a different lab. They did (hyper +0.28, hypo −0.21) → it replicates.
In plain EnglishAUC is a 0–1 score for how well the markers tell tumor from normal; 1.0 = perfect, 0.5 = coin-flip. Cross-validation tests the model on data it never saw during training, so a high score can’t come from memorizing.
PCA plot. Each dot is a sample squashed onto two summary axes; tumor and normal fall into clearly separate clouds — a visual echo of the 0.996 separation.

7. Silencing vs. activation — what those words mean

In plain EnglishHyper-methylation in a gene’s promoter = the on-switch gets chemically crowded, blocking the machinery that reads the gene → the gene is silenced (off). Hypo-methylation = the on-switch is left clear → the gene can be activated (on). We only make this call inside promoters, because outside them methylation can mean the opposite — those we mark ambiguous and don’t over-claim.

Honest note on “pathways.” When we tested whether whole longevity pathways (telomere, stem-cell, FGF/IGF/Wnt…) are enriched, none survived the field-standard bias correction — the initial hits were an artifact of the array design (bigger genes carry more probes and look enriched by chance). So the confident findings are at the site level (specific genes like FGF2), not the broad pathway level. Reporting this as a negative result is the honest, stronger position.
